# Giant gas permeance through a nanoporous crystalline 2D polymer

**Authors:** Jonathan D'Emidio, Daniel J. Murray, William B. Thompson, Jarvis C. Humlick, K. C. Schwab, Adrian Del Maestro, Benjamin T. King

Chemical separation processes represent one of the largest contributors to industrial energy consumption, underscoring the need for the most efficient separation strategies. Many separation processes rely on selective transport through porous media. In this context, two-dimensional (2D) materials offer a promising route to atomically precise porosity beyond the reach of traditional top-down fabrication, with the potential to enhance separation performance by increasing flow. Here we report measurements of gas transport through a one-molecule-thick layer of suspended, nanoporous poly(carboxyfantrip), a 2D polymer that has identical, nanometer-sized pores that cover nearly a quarter of the surface. This combination of ultimate thinness with high areal pore density gives normalized gas flows (permeances) that approach the effusion limit, three to four orders of magnitude above gas-sieving graphene membranes. We find that gases spanning nearly two orders of magnitude in molecular weight (H$_2$, He, N$_2$, CO$_2$, and SF$_6$) obey Graham's law and approach the ideal limit of non-interacting ballistic transport, despite strong, species-dependent gas-pore interactions. Simulations reproduce the measured permeances within experimental uncertainty and provide an atomistic picture of the transport process. This work demonstrates the potential of synthetic crystalline 2D polymers for chemical separation, water filtration, and quantum sensing.
